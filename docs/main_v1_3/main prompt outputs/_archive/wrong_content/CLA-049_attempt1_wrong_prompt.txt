Checking the leveraged fund's subtraction

On its own, this report says little about where Treasuries were headed. The leveraged-fund short is almost exactly what your model expected, and the broader pattern across categories is the usual structural one, not a directional bet.

The model residual is noise. Leveraged funds were net short 10.37% of open interest against an expected 9.39%. That −0.98 point gap sits well inside the 90% range (−13.16% to −5.63%). It is also smaller than 63% of earlier out-of-sample misses, so a residual this size is a routine week. There is no evidence here of unusual crowding, capitulation or a positioning extreme.

The category pattern is mostly plumbing:

Asset managers long (+13.9%) and leveraged funds short (−10.4%) is the classic mirror image in Treasury futures. Asset managers often hold futures as cheap, capital-light duration exposure. Leveraged funds often take the other side through the cash-futures basis trade: they buy cash Treasuries and sell futures. A large leveraged-fund short is therefore often hedged, not a bet on higher yields.
Dealers net short (−7.3%) is typical of intermediaries hedging inventory and client flow.
Other reportables net long (+5.9%) is a smaller directional or hedging cohort.
The reportable nets sum to +62,675 contracts. That implies non-reportable traders were net short about 62,700 contracts, roughly 2.1% of open interest. The arithmetic in your table checks out.

How the date fits in. Early February 2016 was a risk-off period, with oil near its lows, equities weak and 10-year yields falling sharply. If leveraged funds had been short far beyond what their usual basis activity explains, you might read that as squeeze risk into a rally. The model says they weren’t, so positioning doesn’t appear to have been a meaningful extra force on yields that week.

Limits on what you can infer:

This is one snapshot. Week-over-week changes, and the run of residuals over several weeks, usually say more than a single level.
The data are futures-only and cover only the 10-year contract. The cash legs of basis trades and positions in other tenors (2s, 5s, bonds, ultras) are invisible here, and duration often moves between contracts.
Positions are as of Tuesday and published Friday, so they are stale for timing purposes.
How informative the residual is depends on what the model already controls for. If it captures basis-trade economics such as repo rates, the implied repo rate and open-interest growth, a small residual means “nothing beyond the usual structure.” If it doesn’t, the expected value may already contain directional views.

In short, positioning that week looks normal for the 10-year market’s structure. It doesn’t support a bullish or bearish conclusion about Treasuries by itself.
