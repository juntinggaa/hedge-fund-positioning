The most interesting feature is the disagreement between asset managers and leveraged funds.

Asset managers are net long approximately 197,000 contracts, while leveraged funds are net short approximately 214,000 contracts. This divergence could support a bullish thesis, especially if leveraged funds eventually cover their short positions.

However, there are three important limitations.

1\. Positioning is not a price forecast. Asset managers may be extending duration exposure for portfolio reasons, while leveraged funds may be implementing relative-value trades rather than outright bearish bets.

2\. The short position is not necessarily extreme. Leveraged funds' net short exposure is 7.06% of open interest. Without several years of historical positioning data, we cannot establish whether this represents an unusually crowded bearish trade.

3\. The report is historical. It describes positions as of November 25, 2014, not current positioning in November 2026. Those positions cannot reliably indicate today's Treasury market conditions.

### What would make me more bullish?

I would look for confirmation from three areas:

- Positioning: Leveraged funds begin reducing their net shorts over multiple reporting weeks.
- Price action: Treasury futures break above a meaningful resistance level, suggesting buyers are gaining control.
- Macro conditions: Falling inflation expectations, declining Treasury yields, or expectations of easier Federal Reserve policy support higher Treasury futures prices.

Remember that Treasury futures prices generally rise when Treasury yields fall, and decline when yields rise.

One final consideration: Treasury futures are leveraged instruments. Even a relatively small rise in yields can produce substantial losses relative to the margin posted.

The most valuable next step would be to calculate the historical percentile or z-score of leveraged funds' net positioning. That would help determine whether their short exposure was genuinely crowded, rather than simply negative.
