This snapshot shows a 10-Year U.S. Treasury Note futures market where Leveraged Funds hold the dominant net long position (+9.04% of open interest), institutional Asset Managers are heavily active on both sides but nearly flat net (+1.23%), and the weekly shift in leveraged-fund positioning landed slightly more net long (+1.73 percentage points) than historical same-week macro and positioning relationships predicted—a deviation well within normal weekly noise.
Cross-Sectional Market Structure (Futures-Only)
Leveraged Funds are distinctly net long: Holding 853,740 long contracts against 498,222 short contracts, leveraged funds carry a net long position of 355,518 contracts (+9.04% of open interest). Unlike periods where hedge funds are heavily net short Treasury futures, they are the primary net long constituency in the 10-year contract here.
Balanced institutional two-way flow: Asset Managers hold the largest gross footprint in the market—1,458,078 longs, 1,409,497 shorts, and 788,616 spreading contracts—yet their net exposure is just +48,581 contracts (+1.23% of OI). Dealers are similarly near flat at -15,629 contracts (-0.40% of OI), pointing to balanced intermediation and diverse institutional positioning rather than a one-sided consensus.
Shorts are concentrated in Other Reportables and Non-Reportables: Other Reportable traders hold a substantial net short of -159,089 contracts (-4.04% of OI). Because the four reportable categories sum to a combined net long of +229,381 contracts (+5.83% of OI), implied Non-Reportable (smaller) traders must hold the offsetting net short of -229,381 contracts (-5.83% of OI) required for the futures market to clear.
What the Model Output Indicates About the Weekly Move
A modestly more bullish/long adjustment than the conditional baseline: Anchored by the prior week's positioning and conditioned on same-week yields, curve shape, volatility, funding proxies, Fed holdings, and open-interest changes, the model estimated that leveraged funds would end the week at +7.31% of OI. The actual outcome (+9.04%) reflects an unexpected weekly adjustment (residual) of +1.73 percentage points of OI (roughly 68,000 contracts) toward the long side.
Routine variation, not a positioning shock: A +1.73 percentage point residual is smaller than the model's out-of-sample RMSE (2.07 percentage points) and sits at the 61st percentile of historical absolute errors—meaning 39% of prior weeks saw a miss at least this large.
Wide uncertainty around weekly flow models: Falling inside the +3.74% to +10.88% interval (a 7.14 percentage point span) does not mean the point estimate tightly anticipated the week's trading. With an out-of-sample $R^2$ of 10.47% against a historical-mean change benchmark, almost 90% of the variance in weekly net position changes remains unexplained by observable market and positioning covariates.
What This Positioning Does Not Tell Us
Net duration or cash-market exposure: CFTC futures-only reports omit cash Treasuries, repo financing, interest rate swaps, and options. Leveraged funds' +9.04% net long in 10-year futures could reflect outright directional exposure, a relative-value curve leg offset by shorts in 2-year, 5-year, or Ultra Bond futures, a swap-spread trade, or a reverse cash-futures basis position against short cash Treasuries.
Strategy breakdown or basis volume: Neither the +7.31% expected level nor the +1.73 percentage point residual measures the size or profitability of the cash-futures basis trade, nor does the residual isolate "discretionary" trading or private information. Both components are reduced-form statistical associations.
Next-week direction: Because model v4.0 relies on current-week open interest and same-week market returns (and was selected retrospectively across data extending to 2026), the +1.73 percentage point residual is a contemporaneous descriptive gap, not a deployable trading signal or a forecast that leveraged funds will mean-revert in the following week.
